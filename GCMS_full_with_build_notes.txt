CHAPTER 1

INTRODUCTION

Project Context

Guidance counseling is a vital process that helps students understand
themselves, make informed decisions, and develop the skills necessary to
handle personal, academic, and social challenges. It supports students’
holistic development by guiding them in achieving their educational and
personal goals. According to Alegado and Alcantara (2021), guidance
counseling contributes significantly to students’ success by enhancing
their self-awareness, personality, and emotional well-being.

At Catanduanes State University (CatSU), the Guidance and Counseling
Office (GCTO) continue to provide services that help students cope with
academic pressure, career uncertainties, and personal concerns. However,
the current walk-in appointment system often leads to scheduling
conflicts, manual record-keeping errors, and longer processing times.
These inefficiencies can delay the delivery of timely support and
increase administrative workload for guidance staff.

To address these challenges, this study proposes the development of a
Guidance Counseling Management System (GCMS) — a web-based platform
designed to automate scheduling, digitalize case documentation, and
generate counseling reports. The system aims to minimize paperwork,
reduce scheduling conflicts, and enhance accessibility to counseling
services. As highlighted by Abiog et al. (2024), adopting digital
systems in guidance offices can significantly improve efficiency, data
management, and service delivery.

Through the GCMS, students will be able to easily book appointments
online, while counselors can manage records and monitor student progress
more effectively. This initiative supports CatSU’s goal of providing
student-centered, technology-driven services that promote academic
success and overall well-being.

Purpose and Description

The Guidance and Counseling Office (GCTO) of Catanduanes State
University (CatSU) currently relies on a manual, walk-in process for
managing student counseling services. This traditional system often
leads to scheduling conflicts, lost records, and delays in assisting
students who require immediate guidance. The manual handling of
appointment forms and counseling data also increases the workload of
guidance staff, making it difficult to efficiently track student
progress and maintain organized records.

To address these limitations, this study aims to develop a web-based
Guidance Counseling Management System (GCMS) that automates the
scheduling and record-keeping processes. The system integrates core
features such as online appointment booking, digital case management,
and automated report generation. By transitioning to a digital platform,
the GCTO can streamline its workflow, ensure data accuracy, and provide
a more organized and efficient service delivery for both students and
counselors.

Furthermore, the system seeks to enhance accessibility and transparency
in guidance operations. Students will be able to schedule counseling
sessions conveniently online, while counselors can monitor and update
case files in real time. The system’s data-driven reporting capability
will also support administrators in evaluating counseling trends and
improving service outcomes. Overall, the GCMS aligns with CatSU’s
commitment to delivering innovative, student-centered services that
promote academic success and personal development.

Objectives

The primary objective of this project is to design and implement an
automated Guidance Counseling Management System (GCMS) that simplifies
the process of booking and managing counseling sessions.

Specifically, this study aims to:

1.  Develop an intuitive and efficient web-based scheduling system that
    automates appointment booking for students and counselors.

2.  Implement digital case management for accurate record-keeping and
    monitoring of counseling sessions.

3.  Generate reports summarizing counseling activities to support
    decision-making and service evaluation.

4.  Evaluate the system’s quality and effectiveness using the ISO/IEC
    25010 software quality model, focusing on functionality, usability,
    performance efficiency, reliability, and security.

Scope and Limitations

The scope of this study covers the design, development, and
implementation of a web-based Guidance Counseling Management System for
CatSU. The system’s core modules include appointment scheduling,
counselor case management, and report generation. It caters to three
user roles: students, counselors, and administrators. The GCMS provides
online booking features, automated notifications, and centralized data
storage to enhance accessibility, efficiency, and service monitoring.
The study is limited to the system’s technical and operational aspects
within CatSU. It does not measure the psychological effectiveness of
counseling sessions but focuses on the functionality and usability of
the platform. The system’s deployment is site-specific, meaning findings
may not fully apply to other institutions. Furthermore, its performance
depends on the university’s IT infrastructure and the users’
adaptability to digital systems.

Definition of Terms

This section defines key terms used in the study to ensure clarity and
consistency in interpreting the concepts and processes involved in the
Guidance Counseling Management System.

  Case Management – A system feature that allows counselors to document,
  organize, and monitor students’ counseling histories and progress.

  Compatibility – The ability of the system to function effectively with
  other software, hardware, or environments.

  Functional Suitability – The extent to which the system provides
  functions that meet the specified user needs.

  Usability – The ease with which users can learn, navigate, and
  interact with the system.

  Performance Efficiency – The system’s ability to perform tasks quickly
  and effectively while utilizing resources optimally.

  Reliability – The consistency of the system’s performance over time
  without failures.

  Security – The capability of the system to protect information and
  prevent unauthorized access.

  Maintainability – The ease of modifying or updating the system to
  correct issues or enhance performance.

  Portability – The ability of the system to be deployed or adapted
  across different environments or platforms.

  ISO/IEC 25010 – An international standard for evaluating software
  quality through eight key characteristics: functional suitability,
  performance efficiency, compatibility, usability, reliability,
  security, maintainability, and portability.

CHAPTER 2

REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE/SYSTEMS

This chapter presents a review of related literature, studies, and
technologies relevant to the development of the Guidance Counseling
Management System (GCMS). It discusses various concepts, models, and
previous research that provide the foundation for understanding how
guidance and counseling services can be improved through digital
innovation. The review covers studies on the management and organization
of guidance services, the integration of information and communication
technologies in counseling systems, and the evaluation of web-based
platforms that enhance accessibility and efficiency. By examining both
local and international research, this chapter identifies the trends,
similarities, and gaps in existing systems, which serve as the basis for
developing a localized GCMS tailored to the needs of Catanduanes State
University.

Guidance Counseling Management System

Kartadinata (2022) conducted a study titled Quality Improvement and
Management System Development of School Guidance and Counseling
Services, published in the Journal of Universitas Negeri Malang. The
study developed and evaluated a model to improve the quality of guidance
and counseling services across various school levels. By introducing a
computerized management framework, the research highlighted the
significance of systematizing guidance services through structured
procedures and digital recordkeeping. This aligns with the present
study’s objective to develop a centralized digital system that supports
efficient management of counseling sessions and student information.

Fataroni (2023), in Implementation of Guidance and Counseling Service
Management in an Effort to Improve the Quality of Student Learning from
Bale Literasi, examined how the management functions of planning,
organizing, leading, and controlling contribute to improving student
learning outcomes. The study emphasized that proper management of
guidance services enhances not only student performance but also
counselor efficiency. This is closely related to the current study,
which also focuses on improving management efficiency by automating
routine tasks in the counseling office.

Similarly, Karunia et al. (2023) in Management Strategies for Guidance
and Counseling Services in Schools to Support Student Achievement,
published in Manajia, presented a literature-based analysis of effective
management strategies for school guidance programs. Their work
underscores the importance of well-defined processes, counselor
coordination, and record organization. These insights support the
rationale for developing a structured information system like the GCMS
to ensure organized service delivery.

Furthermore, Jannah, Neviyarni, and Sukur (2022) in The Role of
Counselors in Developing Guidance and Counseling Management in Schools
from Quality Journal, explored how counselors contribute to designing
and maintaining guidance management systems. The authors found that
counselor involvement in system development improves implementation and
user acceptance. This supports the inclusion of counselors and staff
feedback in the design process of the proposed GCMS, ensuring that the
system meets actual user needs.

Technologies Integrated in GCMS

Nasution et al. (2024), in Integration of Information Technology in
Guidance and Counseling Management in Schools: Challenges and
Implementation Strategies published in Attanwir Journal, discussed the
integration of IT into guidance counseling management. The study
identified challenges such as limited digital literacy, technical
constraints, and resource availability, but emphasized that proper
training and infrastructure lead to more efficient service delivery.
This finding supports the technological framework of the proposed GCMS,
which integrates IT tools to simplify administrative tasks.

Majjate et al. (2024) proposed an AI-Powered Academic Guidance and
Counseling System Based on Student Profile and Interests, published in
MDPI. Their system utilized artificial intelligence to recommend
personalized academic guidance. Although more advanced than the proposed
GCMS, it demonstrates the growing trend of using intelligent
technologies to improve student support services. This reinforces the
idea that automation and data-driven systems enhance the responsiveness
and accuracy of guidance operations.

Azmin (2023), in Integrating ICT into Career Counseling: An Empirical
Study of the TACC Digital Content Model in Malaysian Secondary Education
from HRMars, investigated the integration of ICT in delivering career
counseling services. The study found that digital models improve student
engagement and counselor productivity. This supports the use of web
technologies in your GCMS, which also aims to increase accessibility and
user interaction.

In addition, Setyawan et al. (2023) in Design of Continuous Web App:
Guidance and Counseling Management Information System at SMKN 1
Purwakarta using Laravel Framework, published in Lembaga KITA, developed
a dynamic GCMS using the Laravel framework. The study’s design approach
and use of modern web tools closely parallel the methodology and
technologies of your own system.

Likewise, Balmores et al. (2024) created an Online Individual Inventory
and Counseling Information System for Cagayan State University–Lasam,
which digitized student records and counseling forms. Their work
demonstrated that a localized web-based system effectively streamlines
information management and reduces manual documentation — an outcome
that directly parallels the goals of your proposed GCMS.

Evaluation of Web-Based GCMS

Sudrajat et al. (2024), in Evaluating Technology Acceptance in
Counseling Services: An Analysis of Principal Indicators Utilizing the
UTAUT2 Model via SEM-PLS, published in EJournal UPI, examined factors
affecting technology acceptance among counselors and administrators. The
study concluded that performance expectancy and ease of use strongly
influence user adoption of digital counseling platforms. This evaluation
model can guide future assessments of your GCMS’s usability and
acceptance.

Guntara et al. (2023), in Web-Based Counseling Skills Evaluation
Information System Using Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM)
Approach, published in IJADIS, developed and assessed a system to
evaluate counselor performance. Their use of the DSRM framework
demonstrates how system development and evaluation can occur in
iterative stages — similar to your project’s methodological structure.

Lastly, Bato and Marcial (2016), in Students’ Attitudes Towards the
Development of an Online Guidance Counseling System from IITTL Journal,
analyzed student perceptions toward web-based counseling platforms.
Findings revealed positive attitudes toward digital accessibility and
convenience. This supports the user-centered design of your GCMS, which
aims to enhance student engagement through an online interface.

Synthesis

The reviewed studies collectively emphasize the growing importance of
technology in improving the management and delivery of guidance and
counseling services in educational institutions. Under the first
thematic area, researchers such as Kartadinata (2022), Fataroni (2023),
and Karunia et al. (2023) highlighted that effective management systems
enhance counselor performance, improve service organization, and
positively influence student learning. Their findings suggest that
structured guidance management, when supported by clear planning and
documentation, leads to more efficient and responsive services.
Likewise, Jannah et al. (2022) underscored the crucial role of
counselors in developing and maintaining such systems, pointing out that
user involvement ensures better implementation and sustainability.
Together, these studies show that the modernization of counseling
services depends not only on management strategies but also on the
active participation of those who use the system.

Studies under the second thematic area demonstrate that integrating
technology into counseling operations brings substantial benefits but
also introduces new challenges. Nasution et al. (2024) and Azmin (2023)
both noted that information and communication technologies improve
accessibility, accuracy, and efficiency in service delivery, while also
requiring adequate training and infrastructure. Majjate et al. (2024)
showed the potential of artificial intelligence to personalize
counseling, while Setyawan et al. (2023) and Balmores et al. (2024)
developed web-based systems using frameworks such as Laravel and
PHP/MySQL to digitalize appointment scheduling and record management.
These studies illustrate the steady shift toward digital transformation
in guidance services, proving that technological integration enhances
both counselor workflow and student engagement.

Meanwhile, the third thematic area centers on the evaluation and user
acceptance of web-based guidance systems. Sudrajat et al. (2024) applied
the UTAUT2 model to examine user acceptance, showing that perceived
usefulness and ease of use significantly influence adoption. Guntara et
al. (2023) employed a design science research approach to evaluate
system effectiveness and counselor performance, while Bato and Marcial
(2016) explored positive student attitudes toward online counseling
platforms. These studies confirm that web-based systems are generally
well-received and effective when designed around user needs and tested
through structured evaluation methods.

Despite these advancements, a noticeable research gap remains. Most
existing systems focus on either specific counseling functions or the
evaluation of technology use but rarely combine both development and
implementation in a localized academic context. Few studies address the
unique challenges of integrating an all-in-one digital platform for
managing counseling schedules, student cases, and reports within a
single institution. The present study addresses this gap by developing a
comprehensive, web-based Guidance Counseling Management System (GCMS)
tailored for Catanduanes State University. Unlike previous works, it
unifies administrative management, appointment scheduling, and
documentation processes into one accessible system—providing an
innovative, student-centered solution that modernizes the university’s
guidance operations.

in creating the Guidance Counseling Management System (GCMS) for
Catanduanes State University. It details the requirements analysis,
documentation, design of software and systems, development, testing,

software development methodology applied in this project is the Agile
Methodology, which emphasizes iterative development, collaboration with
stakeholders, and continuous improvement. Agile was selected because it
allows flexibility in addressing user feedback and ensures that the
system is aligned with the needs of the Guidance Counseling and Testing
Office (GCTO).

Requirements Analysis

The requirements were gathered through multiple techniques, including
interviews with guidance counselors, surveys with students, and direct
observations of existing counseling processes at CatSU. The current
process is largely paper-based, requiring students to manually fill out
consent forms and book counseling appointments through walk-ins. This
creates inefficiencies such as scheduling conflicts, missed
appointments, and difficulties in record management.

The proposed system addresses these issues by introducing a digital
platform for booking appointments, managing counseling records, and
generating reports. The data collected through requirement analysis
provided the foundation for both functional and non-functional
requirements

Figure2 Context Diagram

The context diagram of the Guidance Counseling Management System (GCMS)
for Catanduanes State University illustrates the system as a single
process and shows its interaction with three primary external entities:
the Student, the Counselor, and the Admin. This diagram establishes the
system boundary and clarifies how data flows between the users and the
system, thereby providing a high-level view of its functionality.

The Student is one of the key actors in the system. Students interact
with the GCMS by registering or logging in, managing their personal
information, and submitting counseling requests. They may also select or
reschedule appointments based on their availability and receive
important system outputs such as appointment confirmations, reminders,
and counseling reports. This interaction ensures that students can
conveniently access guidance services without relying on manual forms or
walk-in requests, making the process more efficient and
student-centered.

The Counselor represents the professional users of the system.
Counselors access the GCMS to view submitted appointment requests,
approve or adjust schedules, and conduct counseling sessions. After each
session, they input session notes, progress updates, and recommendations
into the system, which are stored as counseling records. They also have
the ability to generate reports summarizing student progress and session
outcomes. By digitizing these tasks, the system reduces the paperwork
load of counselors and allows them to focus more on delivering effective
guidance to students.

The Admin serves as the system overseer. Administrators log in to the
GCMS to manage user accounts, configure system settings such as
scheduling policies or notification rules, and monitor system activity
through logs and generated reports. They are responsible for ensuring
that the system operates smoothly and securely, while also using the
aggregated data for decision-making and policy development. The system
provides admins with outputs such as user activity logs, statistical
reports, and summaries of counseling trends, which are essential for
evaluating the performance of the guidance services.

The arrows in the diagram represent the data flows between each external
entity and the system. Inputs include login credentials, appointment
requests, counselor notes, and configuration data, while outputs consist
of confirmations, notifications, reports, and monitoring feedback. By
clearly showing these exchanges, the context diagram highlights how the
GCMS acts as a centralized platform that integrates the needs of
students, counselors, and administrators.

In summary, the context diagram emphasizes that the GCMS is designed to
streamline the overall counseling process at CatSU by reducing manual
workloads, ensuring accurate record-keeping, and providing timely access
to guidance services. It portrays the system as the central hub where
all users interact, ultimately improving the efficiency and
effectiveness of guidance counseling operations within the university.

SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

[]

Figure 7 System Architecture

The system architecture of the Guidance Counseling Management System
(GCMS) follows a three-tier structure that ensures a seamless, secure,
and efficient interaction between users and the application’s core
functionalities. At the forefront of this architecture are the users,
who may take on different roles such as students, counselors, or
administrators. These users interact with the system through a Graphical
User Interface (GUI), which serves as the visual and interactive layer
of the application. Through this interface, users can perform a range of
tasks such as registering an account, updating their profile, booking or
rescheduling counseling appointments, and accessing assigned counselor
sessions.

When a user initiates an action, the GUI captures the input and
communicates it to the web server, which in this case is powered by
Apache. The web server functions as the intermediary between the user
interface and the database. It processes incoming requests, executes the
appropriate logic, and determines what kind of response or data is
required. For example, if a student wants to view their scheduled
counseling session, the web server interprets this request, applies the
necessary business rules, and prepares a query for the database.

The final tier of the architecture is the database, built on MySQL,
which acts as the central repository for all information in the system.
This includes user account details, counseling schedules, student
records, counselor session notes, notifications, and administrative
data. The web server queries the database to either retrieve or update
this information based on the user’s interaction. Once the operation is
completed—whether it is fetching scheduled appointments or saving a new
counseling record—the database sends the result back to the server,
which then communicates the outcome to the GUI for the user to see.

This three-tier architecture ensures that the GCMS operates with
efficiency, scalability, and security, while maintaining a clear
separation of concerns between the presentation layer (GUI), application
logic (web server), and data management (database).

Development and Testing

Software Development Method

For the development of the Guidance Counseling Management System (GCMS),
the researchers adopted the Agile Methodology to ensure that the system
was built in an iterative and user-centered manner. Unlike traditional
linear models, Agile allowed flexibility in refining the system features
based on the feedback of its intended users—students, counselors, and
administrators of CatSU. Through incremental sprints, the essential
modules such as appointment scheduling, counselor session management,
and administrative account control were developed, tested, and improved
until they met the identified requirements of the study.

In addition to development, this chapter also focuses on the evaluation
of the GCMS. To assess the system’s quality and acceptability, the
researchers employed the Descriptive Survey Method guided by the ISO/IEC
25010 software quality model. This framework measures software through
eight key characteristics, including functionality, usability,
reliability, and security. The evaluation results provided a clear
indication of how well the system addressed the needs of its users and
whether it could be adopted effectively within the university.

Figure21 Agile Methodology

The development of the Guidance Counseling Management System (GCMS) was
carried out using the Agile Methodology. Unlike traditional linear
models, Agile emphasizes flexibility, collaboration, and incremental
progress. This methodology was chosen because it allowed the researchers
to continuously refine the system based on user feedback from students,
counselors, and administrators. Agile ensured that the GCMS evolved
according to the real needs of its end-users while reducing risks
associated with rigid, one-time development.

At the start of the development process, the researchers conducted
requirement gathering and user story creation. Through interviews,
surveys, and consultations, the team identified the challenges in the
existing manual guidance counseling process. These requirements were
translated into user stories such as: “As a student, I want to schedule
or reschedule an appointment so that I can meet my counselor at a
convenient time” and “As a counselor, I want to record session notes so
that I can maintain accurate student records.” These user stories served
as the foundation for prioritizing features in each Agile iteration or
sprint.

The planning phase involved selecting the most essential features for
the first sprint. High-priority functions such as user authentication,
appointment scheduling, and counselor viewing of appointments were
chosen because they formed the core of the system’s operations. This
allowed the researchers to deliver a working prototype early in the
development process, which could immediately be tested and evaluated by
potential users.

During the development phase, the system was built incrementally. The
team worked on one sprint at a time, developing modules such as Student
Registration and Login, Appointment Scheduling, Counselor Session
Management, and Admin User Account Management. After each sprint, the
partially completed system was demonstrated to users for feedback. This
approach provided the flexibility to make changes based on suggestions,
such as refining the user interface to be more intuitive or adjusting
the flow of appointment notifications.

Testing was integrated into every sprint, ensuring that features were
validated as soon as they were developed. Unit testing checked the
correctness of individual modules, while integration testing ensured
that modules worked together seamlessly. For example, the process of
scheduling an appointment was tested end-to-end to confirm that the
request made by a student was properly displayed on the counselor’s
dashboard and stored in the database. Bugs identified during testing
were immediately corrected within the sprint before moving on to the
next set of tasks.

The review and retrospective phase followed each sprint. In this phase,
feedback was gathered from the stakeholders who interacted with the
system. Students evaluated whether the scheduling feature was
convenient, counselors checked the usability of session record
management, and administrators tested the account management functions.
The researchers discussed what went well, what challenges were
encountered, and what improvements were needed in the next sprint.

Finally, the deployment phase was conducted after several completed
sprints resulted in a functional and stable version of the GCMS. The
system was deployed for trial use at CatSU, allowing real users to
interact with it in an actual environment. Maintenance activities
followed, focusing on minor refinements and enhancements based on
additional feedback. Since Agile is iterative by nature, the system
remains open for continuous improvement and expansion even after
deployment.

Through the use of Agile Methodology, the development of the GCMS was
made more adaptable and user-centered. The iterative sprints ensured
that the system’s features were tested and validated continuously,
reducing risks of failure. More importantly, Agile enabled active
participation of students, counselors, and administrators throughout the
development process, ensuring that the final product was aligned with
their needs and expectations.

Evaluation

The evaluation of the Guidance Counseling Management System (GCMS) was
conducted to measure its overall quality, usability, and acceptability
among its intended users. To accomplish this, the researchers employed
the Descriptive Survey Method, which made use of a structured
questionnaire distributed to students, counselors, and administrators
who were identified as the primary users of the system. The survey
instrument was developed based on the ISO/IEC 25010 software quality
model, which provides an internationally recognized framework for
assessing software quality.

The evaluation focused on the eight characteristics outlined in ISO/IEC
25010. Functional suitability was assessed to determine whether the GCMS
delivered the necessary features expected by its users, such as student
appointment scheduling, counselor record management, and administrative
control of accounts. Performance efficiency measured the responsiveness
of the system in processing login requests, scheduling transactions, and
updates to the database. Compatibility referred to the ability of the
system to operate seamlessly across different devices and web browsers
commonly used in the university.

Usability was another crucial aspect, which examined the system’s user
interface, navigability, and ease of use for first-time users and
frequent users alike. The reliability of the GCMS was assessed in terms
of its ability to consistently perform its intended functions without
system crashes or data errors, particularly during multiple simultaneous
transactions. Security was evaluated to ensure that the system provided
adequate protection for sensitive information, preventing unauthorized
access to student and counselor records.

In addition, maintainability was measured to determine how easily the
GCMS could be updated or modified when improvements or corrections were
necessary. Finally, portability was considered in terms of the system’s
ability to adapt to different environments or be deployed in other
academic institutions with minimal adjustments.

The results of the survey were tallied, analyzed, and interpreted to
establish the system’s overall acceptability. Based on user responses,
the GCMS was found to be highly functional and reliable, with a
user-friendly interface that met the needs of its intended users. Areas
for improvement were also identified, particularly in enhancing system
performance efficiency and strengthening security measures. These
findings served as the basis for future refinements of the system.

gathering data to support the development and evaluation of the Guidance
Counseling Management System (GCMS). During the development phase, data
were collected through interviews, observations, and reviews of existing
documents from the Guidance Office of CatSU. These activities provided
the researchers with a clear understanding of the current manual process
of managing appointments, counseling records, and student information.
The insights gained from this stage allowed the researchers to identify
the problems faced by students, counselors, and administrators, which
were then translated into system requirements and user stories that
guided the iterative development under the Agile methodology.

For the evaluation phase, the study employed a survey questionnaire
anchored on the ISO/IEC 25010 software quality model. The instrument was
validated by experts and administered to a sample group consisting of
students, counselors, and administrators who tested the system during

on eight quality characteristics: functional suitability, performance
efficiency, compatibility, usability, reliability, security,
maintainability, and portability. Respondents rated each item using a
Likert scale, and the gathered data were tallied, analyzed, and
interpreted to determine the overall quality and acceptability of the
system.

Through this two-pronged data gathering approach, the study was able to
capture both the requirements for development and the metrics for
evaluation, ensuring that the GCMS was not only responsive to user needs
but also aligned with international software quality standards.

GUIDANCE COUNSELING MANAGEMENT SYSTEM (GCMS) - BUILD & DESIGN NOTES

Prepared for: Jasper

1. SYSTEM OVERVIEW — HOW THE WHOLE SYSTEM WORKS
This capstone is a centralized digital platform that replaces CatSU’s manual walk-in counseling process.

Actors:
- Students
- Counselors
- Admin

Everything revolves around appointments, counseling sessions, and student records.

Core logic:
1. Students log in → request appointment → fill wellness form (optional)
2. Counselor reviews schedule → accepts/declines → conducts session → writes notes
3. Admin oversees everything → users → schedules → reports
4. System saves everything in a secure database
5. Notifications are sent for appointments, updates, and reminders

2. FULL SYSTEM LOGIC (DETAILED)

A. Student-Side Logic
1. Authentication
- Student logs in using email/password
- System checks `users` table
- Role must be `student`
- System loads student dashboard

2. Student profile
- Can update profile
- Stored in `students` table
- Linked to user account by `user_id`

3. Appointment Booking
- Student picks preferred counselor
- Student selects date/time
- System checks counselor availability:
  - No double-booking
  - No overlapping session
- If available → create record in `appointments` table
- Status = "pending"

4. Appointment Notifications
- Student receives notification
- Counselor receives notification
- Stored in `notifications` table

5. View Appointment Status
- Student sees: Pending / Approved / Declined / Completed

B. Counselor-Side Logic
1. Authentication
- Login via `users` table
- Role must be `counselor`

2. Appointment Management
- View pending appointments
- Accept or decline
- If accepted → status becomes "approved"
- If declined → student notified

3. Counseling Session
During the appointment:
- Counselor views student record
- Adds session notes
- Adds recommendations
- Option to add referrals
- System stores them in `counseling_records`

4. Past Sessions
- Counselor sees student history
- Helps with follow-up counseling

C. Admin-Side Logic
1. Authentication
- Login with admin credentials
- Role must be `admin`

2. User Management
- Create students
- Create counselors
- Deactivate accounts
- Grant/Remove access roles

3. System Configuration
- Opening hours
- Notification settings
- Appointment policies

4. Monitoring Dashboard
- Total students
- Total counselors
- Total appointments
- Pending approvals
- Sessions completed

5. Reports
- Counseling frequency
- Counselor loads
- Student records
